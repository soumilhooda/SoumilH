Three Clocks

Slow weekend. I had travel to pack for and a film presentation to attend. Midway through it, an article arrived. I forwarded it around; Hrushikesh replied almost immediately, annoyed. His questions deserved a proper answer.

They're acting like Ken Griffin just saved the whole industry.

I still don't get it. If SA had $16B in public assets and it was running 4x leverage, where did the $45B valuation before the Citadel deal come from?

And they said it did 400% in the last half year. So even on his margin that's an $80B fund his $16B grew into. Include the whole position, 500% of his assets, and it's cartoonishly more.

-Hrushikesh Reddy

Situational Awareness is a miniature of the larger confusion, which is best understood through three clocks: algorithms improve in months, data centres and grids take years to build, and markets reprice their financing every morning.

SA was launched in 2024 with less than a billion dollars under the former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner, on a single wager: that AI scaling would demand an industrial buildout of chips, memory and power, and that the securities of the firms supplying it were the cleanest way to own a piece of the pie. The book was concentrated: long the infrastructure, short a basket of software names thought vulnerable to the same technology, and carried at roughly four times leverage. In the final week of July the longs fell heavily while the shorts moved against the book from the other side, and the prime brokers, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Bank of America, called for collateral. Rather than raise fresh capital the fund sold its entire public portfolio to Citadel at a discount of more than a tenth to the already-marked-down prices, repaid the lenders, and kept what sat outside the leveraged accounts [Reuters][HFA]. The letter to investors put July at -67% and the year at +80%, with all leverage removed [Reuters].

(1 + 4.45) × (1 − 0.67) = 5.45 × 0.33 ≈ 1.80  →  +80% for the year

A gain of 445% followed by a loss of two-thirds leaves him up about 80%.

The reported figures cannot be placed on one balance sheet without more information. A 445% return turns each dollar of opening equity into $5.45; it does not, by itself, explain how a fund launched with less than $1bn reached a reported size of roughly $45bn. That figure must also reflect later subscriptions, a different definition of fund size, leverage, or some combination of the three. What is clear is that the public book was concentrated and leveraged, and that falling longs, rising shorts and higher collateral demands forced the fund to sell approximately $16bn of public positions.

A prime broker lends against positions and continually recalculates the collateral required to support them. When quoted prices fall, the broker can demand cash or reduce the permitted exposure; sales made to meet that demand can push prices lower and trigger further calls. The private Anthropic stake was outside the margined public-equity book and was therefore not exposed to the same immediate liquidation loop. Private assets can still be revalued or pledged, but the location of the stake, rather than merely the absence of a daily quoted price, is what mattered.

Citadel did not rescue the industry. It removed one forced seller and was paid to absorb the inventory, taking the block more than a tenth below its already reduced marks [CNBC]. Griffin bought liquidity cheaply. He did not buy a view on 2030, and the deal answered none of the questions that had produced the selling. A $16bn purchase did not replace the roughly $3tn erased from semiconductor market values.

SA died where the three clocks meet. A model can become cheaper within a quarter; the power connection for the data centre serving it can take years; and the market's price of financing changes every day. For a hedge fund, that repricing arrives immediately through marks and collateral calls. For a data-centre owner, it arrives through higher refinancing costs and stricter returns on the next project. The common problem is duration mismatch: a thesis measured in years exposed to financing conditions measured in days.

“Trillions on AI” means nothing until you cost one unit. Take a gigawatt of data centre as a bill of materials.

Contract prices for Hopper-class parts rose about 40% between October 2025 and March 2026 on memory pass-through alone [IntuitionLabs]. The annual cost of the same gigawatt swings from about $7bn to about $12bn depending on whether a server remains productive for seven years or three [Epoch]. Extending the accounting life reduces annual depreciation, but it does not guarantee that the hardware will remain economically productive for that long. Each new accelerator generation can lower the cost of compute enough to shorten the useful earning life of the previous one. The depreciation schedule therefore embeds a forecast about the speed of technological obsolescence.

The rest of the block is slow and physical, and no amount of money can remove every constraint. Transformer lead times can exceed 50 weeks; of the roughly 16 GW of US capacity announced for 2026, about 5 GW is actually under construction, and between one-third and one-half of the announced pipeline may be delayed or cancelled [Axis]. At near $60bn/GW, the 25GW to 35GW everyone quotes is $1.5-2tn [Orennia], a number large enough to be repeated reverently and mixed enough to mean almost nothing, since it lumps projects with different contracts, counterparties, power and residual values into one figure the financial system is supposed to fund as if it were one thing.

Renting a GPU by the hour costs far more than the rates customers locked in on multi-year contracts in 2024 and 2025. As those contracts expire and reset, the owners re-price up toward the marginal rate, cash flow accelerates, and the money for the next build comes from their own operations rather than new debt. So the widening in credit spreads is an over-reaction to a funding problem already easing. If compute is genuinely scarce, the owners hold more pricing power than their accounts show.

An H100 rents for roughly $1.5-4 a GPU-hour on the neoclouds and $7-12 on hyperscaler on-demand, while a three-year commitment runs 45-60% below on-demand [IntuitionLabs]. I do not think this gap can be looked at as hidden profit. Split the on-demand price in two:

Pon-demand = Cown + R,  with Cown ≈ $1.45/GPU-hr fully burdened at 70% utilisation

Cown is the owner's fully burdened cost of buying, powering and operating the chip: approximately $1.45 per GPU-hour. That figure combines the amortised price of the accelerator and its server, the electricity and cooling to run them, and a share of the shell, networking and operations, all divided by the hours the chip is actually busy; at lower utilisation the same fixed costs fall on fewer hours and the number rises, which is why the 70% assumption matters. The remainder, R, includes the price of scarcity, flexibility and provider margin [Silicon Analysts]. The hourly customer pays for the right to walk away tomorrow. A customer signing for three years gives up that flexibility, accepts utilisation and obsolescence risk, and receives a lower rate in exchange. Baker's argument therefore becomes weaker if spot prices are treated as a direct forecast of contract renewals. Renewals may rise while remaining far below spot rates, particularly because customers can optimise models, shift routine workloads, change providers or substitute custom silicon.

Operating cash flow is the cash generated by the existing business before capital expenditure and financing. It is therefore a genuine source of funding, but not automatically spare funding: the same cash must also support maintenance investment, taxes, dividends, buybacks and the current construction programme. Across the five largest cloud companies, operating cash flow is growing about 23% annually while capital expenditure is growing nearly 70%; on that path, the two cross around Q3 2026 and aggregate free cash flow approaches zero [Epoch]. Their combined 2026 capital budget is close to $700bn, up more than 80%, and could exceed $900bn by 2028 [FactSet]. Baker identifies a source of funds, but not necessarily one large enough to remove the financing constraint.

Whether the spending is repaid depends on demand for compute, and “compute” contains several different workloads. Training is a concentrated expense: a frontier run can occupy a cluster for weeks or months, and an unsuccessful run still consumes most of the electricity and chip-time assigned to it. Post-training then shapes the model through human feedback, synthetic data, reinforcement learning and tool use. Inference has two principal phases. Prefill processes the prompt in parallel and is generally more compute-intensive; decoding generates tokens sequentially and, particularly at smaller batch sizes, is often constrained by memory bandwidth and communication. The economics therefore depend not only on arithmetic throughput but also on how efficiently weights and intermediate states move through memory. Sparse models reduce the number of parameters activated for each token, allowing total model capacity to grow without making inference cost rise in direct proportion.

Reasoning shifts more of the burden onto inference. Frontier models spend additional compute at the moment of use by testing alternatives, checking intermediate results and calling tools before answering. A difficult reasoning task can therefore require many times more inference compute than a direct answer. The economically relevant unit is not the token alone but the completed, verified task, whose price competition should eventually drive down.

Compute spend   S = T · d · p

Better chips and software push the price per unit (p) down, wider use pushes the number of tasks (T) up, and the depth of each task (d) cuts both ways. A better model wastes fewer steps on a task of fixed difficulty, so (d) falls where the task is fixed; but it also makes harder tasks possible, and those burn far more depth, so (d) rises as people attempt more. Which effect wins is open. Spending grows only if the rise in the number and depth of tasks outruns the fall in unit price, only if people use so much more intelligence as it cheapens that they more than make up the cut. They might: cheaper code invites work nobody would have paid to attempt, and an agent reliable enough to drive a browser or watch a network reaches work machines could never touch. But it is hard to measure, benchmarks saturate and mislead, and the value shows up only where a model sits inside an institution that can supervise it, so real adoption lags well behind the benchmark scores.

And the finished task is turning into a commodity. Kimi K3, from Moonshot in Beijing, is a mixture-of-experts model of about 2.8 trillion parameters that fires only a few of them per token, ships with public weights, costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 out, and scores in genuine frontier territory, third on the harder public evals behind only the top closed models [Solvimon]. Frontier-grade intelligence, downloadable, priced to undercut everyone above it. And not a China story: open weights come from DeepSeek, GLM, Qwen and several Western labs too, and they follow a familiar pattern, that making one layer a commodity moves the profit to the layer beneath. An open operating system did not kill computing; it pushed the rent down to hardware, services and distribution. Open weights can crush the labs' margins and, at the same time, raise demand for the memory, networking and inference the models run on. Bearish for the model layer, bullish for the silicon under it, something the market had trouble holding for much of the summer.

Credit is the daily clock, and lately it has behaved like a governor. Start with how eagerly investors are buying the bonds these companies issue. The cover ratio, how many dollars of orders come in for each dollar of bonds offered, fell from nearly 5x in February to under 2x by July; the big cloud borrowers had issued about $194bn by early July, up roughly 79% on all of 2025; and the extra yield they pay over safe government debt widened about 0.1 percentage points across maturities [Reuters]. Credit and equity can point opposite ways at the same time: a project delayed because money got tighter is revenue missing from a supplier's next year, bad for the supplier now, and yet the same restraint means the capacity that does get built is worth more later, good for its owner then. And the strain is concentrated, not general: drop the one BBB-rated borrower whose default insurance jumped from about 0.5% to 2% a year, and the rest, all higher-rated, still borrow near normal investment-grade terms, so the systemic-looking fear is mostly one company [RIA]. The financing has also turned circular in a way that is hard to account for honestly: a chip maker takes a stake in a customer, and the customer then commits to buying that maker's chips, AMD's up-to-$5bn in Anthropic against roughly 2GW of MI450 accelerators from 2027 being the clearest case [Value Add]. Deals like that can be sensible, but they blur real outside demand with demand a supplier paid for, and they should be counted once, not booked as revenue and again as proof the customer is healthy.

CXMT is closing on Micron's memory-wafer output in 2026 and aiming for about 17% of the world's DRAM by 2028 [Tom's Hardware], but its pressure lands on ordinary DDR5 memory. Prices for that ran up about 83% between September 2025 and January 2026 as the incumbents shifted their own lines toward HBM, the high-bandwidth memory stacked next to AI chips [Gasgoo]. HBM, the part the accelerators actually need, stays scarce, and CXMT is a generation or two behind on it, under 2% of its output [SemiAnalysis]. Ordinary memory versus HBM is the line between a threat to the next decade's earnings and a threat to this year's cash. The other risk is the open-weight model, which shortens the time any lab keeps a lead. Neither dents this year's profit; both cut what the future is worth, akin to the July repricing, as SanDisk fell more than 55% and SK Hynix and Nebius close to half in a single month [Benzinga].

A company's market value is not a pot of cash that has to be topped back up dollar for dollar; it is the last price a share traded at, applied to every share outstanding. Sell a small amount lower and the whole is repriced to it. When everyone knows the seller is forced, the drop runs ahead of the sale: buyers pull their bids and other holders sell first, ahead of the liquidation they can see coming, so the price falls before the block even trades. Hand that block to a buyer with more cash and more patience and the overhang is gone at once; the short-sellers buy back, the automated funds stop selling, ordinary buyers return, and a small shift in flow restores a lot of value with nobody having to replace the lost trillions in cash. That is how a $16bn block matters to a market that lost $3tn, and why Citadel could end the forced-selling phase without answering a single question that started it.

The speed of the fall came from the same place. SA looked far more like the old blow-ups than like a bad startup bet: Long-Term Capital Management, whose positions looked diversified until they all moved together under stress; the family offices and volatility funds that found their borrowing terms could sink them faster than their holdings fell. Prime brokers reprice positions continuously and recompute how much collateral they demand; risk systems cut how much a fund is allowed to hold as volatility rises; trend-following funds sell when prices turn and swings widen; options dealers adjust their hedges as prices move; and everyone else, spotting a forced seller from the unusual volume and the way names fall together, trades against the position they can see. None of them needs a view on AI or a belief that the thesis is wrong. Each only needs to see the pressure holding, and each move is reasonable on its own while the sum is a sale no analyst would choose at that price. It feeds on itself: the fall raises measured risk, higher risk shrinks how much can be held, and the forced selling deepens the fall. That is the real shape of a modern crash, not one machine forming a grand opinion about AI, but a swarm of narrow programs each enforcing its own limit at the same time, visible only under stress.

The fund's collapse does not prove that its industrial thesis was wrong. It proves that a concentrated, crowded and leveraged expression of that thesis could not survive a rapid repricing. When an open-weight release, a higher spending forecast and a Chinese-tooling headline arrived together, the financial clock moved before the underlying industrial thesis could be tested. Citadel removed the forced seller and ended the mechanical phase; it did not settle the argument about AI returns.

Intelligence has to become tasks, tasks revenue, revenue cash, and cash a return above the cost of the capital it took to make, and at each step sits a lab, a cloud, a customer, a chip supplier, a lender or a prime broker whose contracts decide who eats the loss when the future shows up late. The technology can be real, the spending cycle can be excessive, and the market can be briefly wrecked by leverage, all three at once. In a real build-out they usually are.
