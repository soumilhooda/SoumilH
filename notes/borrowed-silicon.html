Borrowed Silicon

Last two weeks in the markets have been a bloodbath. Glancing at the numbers every now and then, there is a quick trend I see. CoreWeave is down about 34%, Nebius about 28%, IREN 29%, Applied Digital 23%, with Cipher at 41%. Putting this up against SOXX at a 15% decline and the NASDAQ being down 5%, the neoclouds seem to be doing far worse than the rest of the market.

As an interesting learning exercise I decided to read through some of the borrowing arrangements for CoreWeave. I only did it for one company as that's all time allowed me. CoreWeave's 2031 debt momentarily did yield at 16% with the CDS near 1100bps. Looks like creditors are starting to feel a bit taken for granted. CoreWeave has $24bn in committed delayed-draw facilities, $9bn still undrawn (though at H1's burn rate that doesn't go very far), so the immediate liquidity concerns appear somewhat exaggerated. But they spent $14.1bn cash on equipment in H1 against $3.7bn operating cash flow. The more interesting thing I found was DDTL 5.5, a roughly five-year facility backed by customer contracts averaging three years. So part of the financing depends on what the hardware can earn after those initial contracts expire.

Coming from silicon, where we spend months trying to squeeze out a few more points on perf and power efficiency for successive generations, we end up making life harder for whoever lent money against the last generation of hardware. The market as a whole has also always questioned the accuracy of contracted rental rates to adequately protect against hardware improvements.

The October selloff to me therefore looks less like an immediate liquidity issue and more like investors changing their assumptions about future borrowing. And perhaps that's reasonable. If your growth requires continually purchasing better hardware with borrowed money, the rate at which that money remains available matters almost as much as the demand for compute itself.

As I'm finishing this sat in an airport lounge, Anthropic's S-1 has just come out. Had to go straight to the infrastructure obligations. Up to $44.6bn in agreements with Nscale, but the fees only start once capacity is delivered and accepted. There's even a clause requiring Nscale to seek qualifying financing for the GPUs and facilities. Another $41.3bn with Lambda, of which $34.9bn is subject to the facility lease actually being secured. So a rather large customer contract doesn't necessarily mean the equipment required to fulfil it has been financed.

I was perhaps giving contracted demand rather too much credit without distinguishing it from capacity that's actually been funded and delivered.
